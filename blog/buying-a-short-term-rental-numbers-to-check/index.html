Buying a Short Term Rental: The Numbers to Check First

List & Host team · October 16, 2026

A listing that says a home grosses $80,000 a year on Airbnb tells you almost nothing. Gross is not net, last year is not next year, and a calendar that looks full is not proof that anyone paid. Before you offer on a short term rental, check a handful of numbers yourself. This guide walks through the three that matter most, the gross multiple, proof of occupancy and the real costs, with a worked example and the figures from the homes our team runs in Fort Worth and the Buffalo River region.

1. The gross multiple

The gross multiple is the asking price divided by the last 12 months of gross booking revenue. Gross booking revenue here means what guests paid for the nights plus the cleaning fees, before platform fees, and without taxes.

Example: a home asking $400,000 that booked $55,000 over the last 12 months has a gross multiple of about 7.3. The same home at $550,000 would be 10.0. A lower multiple means you pay less for each dollar the home brings in. Run the same math for a long term rental nearby, using a year of rent, and compare. If the short term multiple is not clearly better, the extra work and risk of nightly guests may not pay for itself.

The multiple is a fast filter, not a valuation. It ignores costs, which is why the next two sections matter more.

2. Proof of occupancy and revenue

Ask the seller for documents, not a summary. In order of value:

  1. Platform exports for 24 months. Airbnb's Earnings page can build a CSV file of paid transactions with gross earnings, host service fees, cleaning fees and taxes withheld for each booking. Vrbo and Booking.com have their own reports. Ask for every platform the home was listed on.
  2. Bank deposits that match the payouts. A payout report and a bank statement that agree are hard to fake.
  3. Booked nights versus available nights, by month. Occupancy is booked nights divided by nights that were open for sale. Ask how many nights the owner blocked for personal use or repairs, because a calendar full of blocked dates looks booked on a screen.
  4. Average nightly rate and average stay length. A home that hit its revenue with deep discounts and one night stays costs more to run than one that got there on full price weekends.
  5. Reviews and badges. Note the review count, the rating and whether the home is a Guest Favorite.
Plan as if the reviews do not come with the house. Listings and reviews usually stay with the seller's host account, so a buyer often starts a new listing with zero reviews. Ask the seller to hide or close the old listing on closing day, and settle any future bookings and their payouts in writing.

Why 24 months, not 12

One year can mislead in either direction. A big event year inflates it, and a year with a long repair hides what the home can do. Seasonality is large even inside one fall. Here is what the homes our team runs did in 2026:

The same home swung from 39% to 77% of nights sold within three months. A seller who shows you only their best quarter is showing you a different business than the one you will run in February. You can see results from the homes our team runs for more of our own numbers.

3. The real costs

Gross revenue loses a large share to costs before anything reaches the owner. These are the lines to price before you offer.

  • Platform fees. Airbnb's single fee is 15.5% of the booking for most hosts on it, and hosts who connect through property management software must use it. On Airbnb bookings at the homes our team runs, the host fee has run from 15.5% to about 16.3% of the booking subtotal, depending on the home. Vrbo and Booking.com set their own commissions; check the rate in each dashboard.
  • Cleaning. One clean per stay. Guests pay a cleaning fee, but you pay the cleaner on every turn. Our guest cleaning fees run from $85 for a small cabin loft to $225 for a home that sleeps 10, and the homes our team runs average 2.7 to 3.4 nights a stay, so turns add up fast.
  • Utilities. You pay them, not the guest. Electricity at our Fort Worth home runs about $200 to $230 a month. Add water, trash, internet and any streaming services.
  • Property tax. Use the seller's last tax bill for the address.
  • Insurance. A standard homeowner policy may not cover paying guests. Get a written quote for a short term rental policy before you offer.
  • Supplies, repairs and replacement. Linens, towels, paper goods and coffee every month, plus repairs and furniture that wears out faster with hundreds of guests a year.
  • Management. If you will not run it yourself, add the manager's fee. Our guide to management fees shows how to compare quotes.

A deal that only works at the seller's best year is not a deal.

Check the building

  • Inspections beyond the standard report. A general home inspection may not cover a well, a septic system, a pool, a hot tub or outbuildings. Order those separately for rural homes.
  • Access. Drive the road in the rain. A steep or gravel entrance affects which guests can arrive and what the listing must say.

Red flags

  • The seller cannot or will not export platform reports.
  • Revenue numbers that come from a projection or a revenue calculator, not bookings.
  • One huge month carrying the year, tied to a one time event.
  • A long run of blocked dates presented as booked.
  • Furniture, linens and supplies not included in the sale.

Quick answers

What is a gross multiple?

The asking price divided by the last 12 months of gross booking revenue. A $400,000 home with $55,000 in bookings has a gross multiple of about 7.3.

Do Airbnb reviews transfer to a new owner?

Plan as if they do not. Listings and reviews usually stay with the seller's host account, so a buyer often starts a new listing with zero reviews.

What occupancy should I expect?

It depends on the market and the season. Across our Fort Worth home and our glass house, the share of nights sold ranged from 39% to 87% month to month between August and October 2026, October as booked on October 16.
