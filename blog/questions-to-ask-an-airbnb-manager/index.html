How to Choose an Airbnb Manager: 15 Questions to Ask

List & Host team · October 18, 2026

To choose a short term rental manager, interview at least three on the same 15 questions, get every answer in writing, and check their proof yourself before you sign. Walk away from any manager who promises a set income, will not write down what the fee is taken from, or wants your listing and reviews in their own account.

We are a management company, so we have a stake in your answer. This is the list we would want an owner to bring to us. The choice matters: hosts in a PriceLabs survey of more than 1,400 spent an average of 8.3 hours a week on their listings (reported November 20, 2025). Still deciding whether to hire anyone? Start with self managing vs hiring a manager.

How to run the search

  1. Shortlist three, including one local team and one larger company. Our comparison of a local team and national managers explains the difference.
  2. Send the same questions by email. Written answers are easier to compare, and they show how clearly each manager writes, which is what your guests will see.
  3. Ask for three things on paper: a sample agreement, a real owner statement with names removed, and links to three listings they run today.
  4. Check the proof yourself with the table below, then score the answers and compare total cost on the same year of bookings.

Fees and money

1. What exactly is your fee taken from?

A percentage means nothing without its base. Fees can come from everything guests pay, from nightly rent only, or from rent after the platform fee, and some agreements count cleaning fees (Awning, updated October 1, 2026). Awning’s own example puts the gap at $1,860 a year on $60,000 of revenue at the same 20% rate, only from taking the fee before or after Airbnb’s 15.5% host fee.

Good answer: one written sentence saying whether platform fees, cleaning fees, taxes and extra guest fees are in or out. Red flag: a rate with no base, or a base that changes between the call and the agreement.

2. What is billed on top, and do you mark anything up?

Setup, linens, supplies, photography, hot tub care and repairs can all be extra. Repairs are usually billed at the vendor’s cost, sometimes plus a coordination markup that varies by company (Awning, October 2026). Our guide to management fees compares two full quotes, extras included.

Good answer: a written list of every possible charge, each marked at cost or with its markup. Red flag: markups that never appear on the statement.

3. Where does guest money go, and what does the statement show?

Some managers let the platforms pay you directly and invoice their fee. Others collect payouts first and pay you the rest. Either can work if the statement ties every payout to a booking, with nights, channel, fees and costs on separate lines.

Good answer: last month’s statement with names removed, and the day of the month it arrives. Red flag: totals only, or “we send a report when you ask.”

Pricing and earnings

4. Who sets my nightly prices, and how often do they change?

Pricing is where a manager earns the fee or wastes it. Rates and minimum stays should move with seasons, weekends, events and how close the date is. Ask who or what sets them, how often your nights are reviewed, and who approves the lowest price your home can take. Our guide to dynamic pricing for owners explains floors and event weeks.

Good answer: a daily review, a floor you approve, event dates priced months ahead and a change log you can see. Red flag: prices set once a season, or “the software handles it” with nobody watching.

5. What do you do when a weekend is still open ten days out?

This shows whether there is a plan or a panic button. A good manager steps open nights down slowly toward a floor and holds event nights firm. A weak one cuts until something books.

Good answer: a specific sequence with a floor, in plain words. Red flag: “we drop it until it books.”

6. What will my home earn, and how did you get that number?

An honest estimate is a range built from homes like yours on real dates, with its basis written down. Nobody can promise what guests will book, and a manager who quotes high to win you still charges the same fee when the year comes in lower.

Good answer: a low to high range, the comparable homes behind it and the date it was pulled. Red flag: a guaranteed income, or one number with nothing behind it.

Guests and reviews

7. Who answers my guests at 2 AM, on holidays and when you are sick?

Airbnb counts response rate as the share of new inquiries and booking requests answered within 24 hours over the past 30 days, and its Superhost program asks for 90% or better plus a 4.8 overall rating (Airbnb Help Center, read October 17, 2026). Treat those as the floor. A guest locked out at night needs an answer in minutes.

Good answer: who covers which hours, who backs them up, and the response rate on a listing they run. Red flag: one person with no backup.

8. Can I see three listings you run right now?

Testimonials on a manager’s own site are easy to write. Live listings are not. In October 2024 Airbnb reported that co hosts in its Co-Host Network averaged 4.9 stars, against 4.62 for large managers with more than 30 active listings, both as of September 30, 2024. Those are group averages, so judge the listings in front of you. Our guide to Airbnb ranking and Guest Favorite explains the badges.

Good answer: links you can open today. Red flag: screenshots only, or “our owners prefer privacy” for every home.

9. How do you handle reviews, and what will you never do for one?

Every good guest should get a review, and every public review a calm reply. Nobody should buy them. Airbnb’s policy says reviews may not be provided or withheld in exchange for something of value, and on August 14, 2024 the Federal Trade Commission announced a rule that bans paying for reviews that express a set opinion.

Good answer: a written review routine, with no discounts or gifts tied to ratings. Red flag: “we give guests a discount for five stars.”

Cleaning, repairs and damage

10. Who cleans, who checks the clean, and who covers when a cleaner cancels?

At the homes our team runs, checkout is 11 AM and check in is 4 PM, so every turn fits in five hours. A missed clean means a bad review and sometimes a refund.

Good answer: a written checklist, photos after every turn and a backup cleaner by name. Red flag: “we will find someone.”

11. What is my repair limit, and who goes to the home in an emergency?

A repair limit is what a manager may spend without asking you. Too low and every small fix waits on your phone. Too high and you learn about big bills from the statement. Also ask how far the nearest team member lives from your home.

Good answer: a written dollar limit, a quote before anything above it, and someone who can reach the home the same day. Red flag: no limit in the agreement, or nobody local for a full service price.

12. How do you document damage and file claims?

Timing decides most claims. On Airbnb, a host asks for damage reimbursement in the Resolution Center within 14 days of the responsible guest’s checkout, with photos and estimates or receipts (Airbnb Help Center, read October 17, 2026). Vrbo and Booking.com have their own processes.

Good answer: photos before cleaning starts, claims filed inside the window, outcomes on your statement. Red flag: no record of what was damaged or lost.

Control and leaving

13. Whose account holds the listing, and what happens to my reviews if I leave?

If the listing lives in your own Airbnb account and the manager joins as a co host, the reviews stay with you, and only you as the listing owner can set up or change a co host’s payouts (Airbnb Help Center, read October 17, 2026). A Vacasa owner guide (read October 17, 2026) says hosts cannot transfer listings on Airbnb or Vrbo, so a listing in the manager’s account stays there and reviews can be moved only by request. Ask the same for Vrbo and Booking.com. Our co host vs property manager guide shows how each model handles the listing.

Good answer: “Your account, your listing. We join as your co host.” Red flag: a new listing built in their account.

14. How long is the term, how much notice ends it, and what does leaving cost?

Terms reported in Awning’s fee guide (updated October 1, 2026) run from no long term contract to 1 to 3 years, with 90 days notice to cancel at one large company. As an illustration only: on $40,000 a year in nightly rent, 90 days of notice at a 20% fee is about $2,000 in fees after you decide to go. Ask who hosts stays booked before the end date.

Good answer: term, notice and any exit fee in one short paragraph, and booked stays honored. Red flag: a long first term with an exit fee and automatic renewal.

15. What do I still control?

You own the home. Ask how you block dates for yourself, whether any fee applies to nights you use, who approves the floor price, and what you can see on your own any day.

Good answer: owner stays with simple notice and no fee, and a dashboard you can open any time. Red flag: limits on your own use of the home.

Red flags that should end the search

  • A guaranteed income, or a number with no comparable homes behind it.
  • A fee with no written base, or charges that show up after you sign.
  • Your listing moved into their account.
  • No live listings you can open and read.
  • Discounts or gifts offered to guests for reviews.
  • No named backup for cleaning or guest messages.
  • Pressure to sign on the first call, or no sample agreement to take home.
  • A long first term with an early exit fee and automatic renewal.

Questions owners ask

What questions should I ask before hiring an Airbnb manager?

Ask what the fee is taken from, what is billed on top, who sets prices, who answers guests at night, whose account holds the listing, and how long the term and notice are.

What are the red flags when choosing a short term rental manager?

A guaranteed income, a fee with no written base, your listing moved into their account, no live listings you can check, discounts for reviews, and a long first term with an exit fee.

How much does a short term rental manager charge?

Full service commonly runs 18% to 25% of revenue, and limited plans about 10% to 15%, according to an Awning fee guide updated October 1, 2026. Always ask what the percentage is taken from.

Should my listing be in my account or the manager's?

Your own account, so the reviews and booking history stay with you if you change managers. On Airbnb, only the listing owner can set up or change a co host's payouts.
