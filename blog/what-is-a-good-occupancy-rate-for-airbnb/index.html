What Is a Good Occupancy Rate for an Airbnb in 2026?

List & Host team · October 18, 2026

A good occupancy rate for an Airbnb in 2026 is one that beats the typical listing in your own market, not a national number. Across the 12 markets we track, the average listing booked 32.6% to 41.0% of its open nights from August 2025 to July 2026, and the top quarter booked about 46% to 62% or more, according to AirROI. But a full calendar at a cheap price can earn less than a half full one priced right, so this guide also sets occupancy beside revenue per open night and what the homes our team runs booked.

Short answer. Find your market's middle listing and its top quarter, then aim for the top quarter on both occupancy and revenue per open night. In AirROI's August 2025 to July 2026 data, that means roughly 50% of nights or more in most getaway markets and about 60% or more in Fort Worth and Dallas. If you book far more than that at a nightly rate below the middle listing's, your price is probably too low.

The national picture

AirROI's table of the 100 largest US markets by listings, updated September 12, 2026, averages 39.7% of nights booked at $315 a night. From the same table, the middle market books 40.3% and the middle nightly rate is about $302; weighted by listings, 39.9% and $317.

  • The range. From 27.6% in Big Bear Lake, California, to 52.0% in Honolulu.
  • Half booked is rare. Honolulu is the only market of the 100 averaging 50% or more. Thirteen average 45% or more, and 19 are under 35%.
  • High rates, fewer nights. Park City, Utah has the top average night at $642 and books 30.3% of nights.
  • Texas. Austin averages 40.0%, San Antonio 38.5%, Dallas 38.4%, Houston 36.1% and Galveston 31.2%.

Rules of thumb call 50% healthy, and AirROI's own FAQ on that page says so (page updated September 12, 2026). Yet 99 of its 100 biggest markets average less. Read 50% as a top quarter result in most places, not a typical one. The middle of our 12 markets, 34.1%, sits below the national middle because nine are getaway markets where weekends and seasons carry the year.

Occupancy against revenue per open night

Revenue per open night is what a listing earns divided by every night it was open to book, booked or not. Data services call it RevPAR. It is roughly the nightly rate times the share of nights booked, so it measures both halves of the job.

No service sees an owner's booking records. Each estimates from what listings show in public and makes different choices:

  • Blocked nights. AirDNA's help center says it divides booked nights by nights not blocked, and counts a listing only with a reservation in the past 28 days. That pushes the rate up; counting every listed night pulls it down.
  • Which listings. Airbnb alone or with Vrbo and Booking.com, 30 night only homes, part time listings.
  • Average or median. AirROI's Fort Worth average is 41.0%; its middle listing books about 43%.
  • Which months. A year ending July 2026 carries the World Cup summer in DFW. One ending April 2026 does not.

So compare your home with one service's numbers for the same period, never one site's average beside another's top quarter. For your own home, divide nights booked by nights you were open to book.

When high occupancy means the price is too low

A calendar that fills fast can mean guests found a bargain. Three signs:

  • Your best nights sell weeks ahead while similar homes stay open at higher prices.
  • The market sells out around you. On October 13, 2026, our weekly comp check found only 7 of 214 similar homes near the Fort Worth home our team runs (3 bedrooms or more, sleeping 6) still open for the weekend of October 23, and 24 of 214 for November 13. Ours had sold both weekends weeks earlier, at about $209 and $248 a night. We raised 23 future weekend nights that day.
  • You book more than your market's top 10% at a rate below its middle listing's. In Fort Worth, that is over 77% of nights at under about $165, AirROI's median rate for the same months.

Our estimator never assumes more than 85% of nights booked; its high cases run about 36% to 51% by market and size. Past 85% there is little room for repairs or owner stays, and the busiest nights are probably underpriced.

Some high occupancy at a low rate is on purpose. The cabin loft our team runs went live September 24, 2026 at $89 on weeknights and $149 on weekends until it has three reviews, and booked 16 of 31 October nights as of October 16. A 30 night stay at a lower rate can fill a slow January with one booking; see our guide to 30 night stays.

Low occupancy can point the other way. At that cabin no weekend night sold at $169; on October 7 we cut October 23 and 24 to $149, and both booked within a week. The fix both ways is pricing by the night: event floors set early, weekends priced on their own, quiet weeknights stepping toward a floor. Our DFW weekend and event pricing guide walks through it.

Questions owners ask

What is a good occupancy rate for an Airbnb?

One that beats the middle listing in your market on both occupancy and revenue per open night. In AirROI's August 2025 to July 2026 data, the top quarter booked at least 46% to 62% of nights across our 12 markets.

What is the average Airbnb occupancy rate in the US?

In AirROI's 100 largest US markets, the middle market books 40.3% of nights, and AirROI's own average is 39.7% (page updated September 12, 2026).

Is 50% occupancy good for an Airbnb?

In most of our markets, yes: it is at or within 2 points of the top quarter line in nine of the 12. Fort Worth (62%), Dallas (59%) and the Buffalo River region (56%) set the bar higher. AirROI data, August 2025 to July 2026.

Can an Airbnb occupancy rate be too high?

Yes. Booking more nights than your market's top 10% while charging less than its middle listing usually means guests are getting a bargain.

Does occupancy count blocked nights?

It depends on who is counting. AirDNA's help center says it leaves blocked nights out. For your own home, divide nights booked by nights you were open to book.
