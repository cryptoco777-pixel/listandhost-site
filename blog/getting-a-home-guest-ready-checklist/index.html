Getting a Home Guest Ready: Checklist You Can Print

List & Host team · October 16, 2026

A guest ready home is one a stranger can open at 4 PM, find everything they need without sending a single message, and leave at 11 AM wanting to come back. That is the bar. This checklist comes from setting up and running the three homes our team runs: a glass house and a cabin loft in the Buffalo River region of Arkansas, and a 3 bedroom home in Fort Worth. Read it once, then use the printable version at the bottom to walk your home room by room.

Start with the five hour window

Many short term rentals run on an 11 AM checkout and a 4 PM check in. All three of ours do. That leaves five hours to strip beds, wash linens, clean, restock and inspect before the next guest arrives. Every item on this list is chosen to make that window easier, not harder.

Three choices matter most. Keep at least two full sets of linens for every bed, so the cleaner makes beds with clean sheets while the used set washes. Store every refill in one locked owner closet, so nothing runs out in the middle of a stay. And give your cleaner a written checklist they can follow without calling you. If your home sleeps eight or more, five hours is tight for one person. Plan for two cleaners on big turns, or a third set of linens so laundry can finish after the next guest checks in.

1. Safety first

Guests never see alarms or wiring in photos, but safety is one of the first things an insurer, a platform or a city inspector asks about, and it is the part of setup that protects people.

  • Smoke alarms. The U.S. Fire Administration says to put smoke alarms inside and outside each bedroom and sleeping area, and on every level of the home, including the basement. Test them monthly and replace each alarm 10 years after the date it was made.
  • Carbon monoxide alarms. If the home has any gas appliance, propane, a fireplace or an attached garage, install CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, as the Consumer Product Safety Commission advises. The CPSC counts more than 200 deaths a year in the United States from accidental carbon monoxide poisoning not related to fire.
  • Fire extinguisher in the kitchen, mounted where a guest can see it. Check the gauge on every turnover.
  • First aid kit in a labeled spot, and a flashlight in each bedroom for power outages.
  • An emergency card with the street address in large print, because a guest calling 911 may not know it. Add the nearest hospital and urgent care, and where the water shutoff and the breaker panel are.
  • Hot tub. A locking cover, posted rules and a set water temperature. Check the water on every turn.
  • Stairs, decks and railings. Walk them yourself and push on every rail. Loose boards and wobbly railings are injuries you can prevent.

2. The essentials Airbnb expects

Airbnb asks every host to provide what it calls essentials: toilet paper, soap for hands and body, one towel and one pillow per guest, and linens for each guest bed. A listing that checks the Essentials box without supplying them can be penalized, up to removal. Treat that list as the floor. Guests compare your home to a good hotel, and the rest of this checklist is what closes that gap.

3. Beds and linens

Sleep is what guests remember. Guests forgive a dated kitchen. They do not forgive a lumpy mattress or a sheet with a stain.

  • A waterproof mattress protector and pillow protectors on every bed.
  • Two pillows per sleeping spot, one firm and one soft.
  • At least two full sets of sheets per bed, and three if you often have same day turns.
  • White or light linens, so stains get seen and treated instead of hidden.
  • An extra blanket in each bedroom closet.
  • Blackout curtains in bedrooms. Night shift workers, light sleepers and families with small children all ask for them.
  • If a sofa or daybed counts as a sleeping spot, say so honestly in the listing and keep bedding for it in the home.

4. Bathrooms

  • Two bath towels, one hand towel and one washcloth per guest, and a bath mat for each shower.
  • Shampoo, conditioner and body wash in refillable pump bottles.
  • Two spare rolls of toilet paper per bathroom, where a guest can see them.
  • A hair dryer, a plunger, and a small trash can with a lid and liners.
  • A dark makeup towel, so the white towels stay white.

5. Kitchen

Stock the kitchen for a family making breakfast and one simple dinner. That covers most stays.

  • Plates, bowls, glasses, mugs and silverware for your guest limit plus two.
  • One large pot, one small pot, two pans, a baking sheet, a cutting board and one sharp knife.
  • A coffee maker and filters, a kettle, a toaster and a microwave.
  • A starter pack: coffee, salt, pepper, cooking oil, dish soap, dishwasher pods, paper towels and trash bags.
  • A fridge that is empty and clean at every check in.

6. Entry and arrival

The arrival is the first review moment. A guest who stands in the dark trying a code three times will write about it.

  • A keypad or smart lock with a new code for every stay. No key under the mat.
  • An outdoor light on a timer or a sensor, so the door is lit after dark.
  • An arrival guide with a photo of the door, where to park and how to get in.
  • For rural homes, directions that work without cell signal and an honest note about the road. If the last stretch is steep or gravel, say so in the listing and again in the check in message. We learned that one at a rural home our team runs.
  • The WiFi name and password on a card inside the home and in the check in message, never in the public listing.

7. The small things guests mention in reviews

  • Fast WiFi. Run a speed test and put the result in the listing.
  • Phone chargers by the beds.
  • A short house manual: checkout steps, trash day, thermostat, TV and hot tub.
  • A few local tips written by a person, not a stack of brochures.
  • Real coffee.

8. Photos and the listing

Book photos only after the home is fully set up, staged and spotless. The first five photos carry most of the decision, so lead with what makes your home different: the view, the hot tub, the big kitchen, the yard. Every bed in a photo should be made with the linens guests will actually get. Then write the listing to match the photos exactly. A guest who finds what the photos promised gives five stars for accuracy, and accuracy is one of the six ratings Airbnb uses to pick Guest Favorites. Our guide to Airbnb ranking and Guest Favorite covers the rest.

9. The turnover routine

A home stays guest ready only with a routine. A good one looks like this after every checkout:

  1. The cleaner is scheduled from the booking calendar as soon as a guest books, not the night before.
  2. On arrival, the cleaner photographs anything damaged or missing before touching it, so a claim can be filed on time.
  3. Beds stripped, linens washed, every surface cleaned, floors done, trash out.
  4. Restock from the locked owner closet against a printed par list.
  5. A final walk: a photo of each room, thermostat set, hot tub covered, doors locked.

Guests pay for the turn through the cleaning fee. Ours run from $85 at the cabin loft to $110 at the Fort Worth home and $225 at the glass house, set by the size of each home and how many guests it sleeps. We run all three on our own pricing and messaging system, with cleaners scheduled around every booking. You can see what that system delivers on our results page.

Common mistakes we see

  • Listing before the home is finished. The first reviews carry the most weight. A missing item in week one costs more than a week of waiting.
  • Too few linens. One set per bed forces same day laundry and rushed cleans.
  • Promising what nature controls. If a view, a creek or a trail depends on the season or the rain, say so plainly.
  • No backup cleaner. Holidays and sick days happen. Line up a second cleaner before you need one.
  • No owner closet. Without one, guests use the spare supplies and the next guest finds none.

Printable guest ready checklist

Print it, walk the home room by room, and check each box.

Safety

  • Smoke alarm inside every bedroom
  • Smoke alarm outside each sleeping area and on every level
  • Every smoke alarm tested this month and under 10 years old
  • Carbon monoxide alarm on every level and outside sleeping areas (gas, propane, fireplace or attached garage)
  • Fire extinguisher in the kitchen, gauge in the green
  • First aid kit in a labeled spot, flashlight in each bedroom
  • Emergency card: street address, nearest hospital, water shutoff, breaker panel
  • Hot tub cover locks, rules posted
  • Stairs, decks and railings solid

Beds

  • Mattress protector and pillow protectors on every bed
  • Two pillows per sleeping spot, one firm and one soft
  • Two full sheet sets per bed, three for back to back turns
  • Extra blanket in each bedroom
  • Blackout curtains in bedrooms
  • Bedding stocked for any sofa bed or daybed you list

Bathrooms

  • Two bath towels, one hand towel, one washcloth per guest
  • Bath mat for each shower
  • Shampoo, conditioner and body wash in pump bottles
  • Two spare toilet paper rolls in sight
  • Hair dryer, plunger, trash can with a lid
  • Dark makeup towel

Kitchen

  • Place settings for your guest limit plus two
  • Pots, pans, baking sheet, cutting board, sharp knife
  • Coffee maker, filters, kettle, toaster, microwave
  • Coffee, salt, pepper, oil, dish soap, dishwasher pods, paper towels, trash bags
  • Fridge empty and clean

Entry and arrival

  • New lock code for this stay
  • Door light works on a timer or sensor
  • Arrival guide with a photo of the door and where to park
  • Honest road note for rural, steep or gravel access
  • WiFi card inside the home, never in the public listing

Extras guests notice

  • WiFi speed tested and stated in the listing
  • Chargers by the beds
  • House manual: checkout steps, trash day, thermostat, TV, hot tub
  • A few local tips written by a person
  • Locked owner closet stocked to the par list

Every turnover

  • Photos of any damage before cleaning starts
  • Beds stripped, linens washed, beds remade
  • Surfaces, floors and trash done
  • Restocked to the par list
  • Final walk: photo of each room, thermostat set, doors locked

Quick answers

How long does it take to get a home guest ready?

A furnished home can usually be ready in a few weeks: supplies, a smart lock, photos and the listing. An empty home takes longer because furniture delivery times vary.

Who pays for cleaning between guests?

The guest, through the cleaning fee you set on each platform. Our guest cleaning fees run from $85 to $225, set by the size of each home.

Do I need a smart lock?

No platform requires one, but self check in with a new code for every stay lets guests arrive at any hour and means no keys to lose or copy.
